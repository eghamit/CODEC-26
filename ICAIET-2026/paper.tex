\begin{abstract}
Residuary (chiefly wave-making) resistance is the performance quantity that
dominates the powering of a hull at speed, yet obtaining it traditionally
requires a physical towing-tank experiment or a costly Reynolds-Averaged
Navier--Stokes (RANS) computation---so few candidate hulls can be evaluated
during early design. This paper benchmarks machine-learning (ML) surrogates that
predict residuary resistance directly from hull-form parameters, trained and
evaluated on \emph{real experimental data}: the Delft Systematic Yacht Hull
Series (DSYHS), \num{308} full-scale towing-tank measurements. Eight regressors
spanning linear, polynomial, instance-based, kernel, ensemble-tree, neural and
Gaussian-process families are compared under a single small-data protocol
(log-target training, ten-times-repeated ten-fold cross-validation). A Gaussian
process (GP) is the most accurate model, reaching a cross-validated coefficient
of determination $R^{2}=\num{0.999}$ and root-mean-square error (RMSE)
\num{0.46} in residuary-resistance units---about a $4.5\times$ reduction in RMSE
relative to the traditional cubic polynomial regression historically fitted to
this series ($R^{2}=\num{0.978}$)---while its \SI{95}{\percent} predictive
intervals are well calibrated (\SI{98.4}{\percent} empirical coverage). We also
find that a distance-based learner ($k$-NN, $R^{2}=\num{0.41}$) fails on this
target, a cautionary small-data result. Exercised as a design tool, the GP
surrogate screens tens of thousands of hull variants per second, recovering a
displacement-versus-resistance Pareto front within the measured design envelope.
The calibrated uncertainty makes the surrogate directly usable in an
active-learning loop that would minimise the number of expensive tank or CFD
evaluations. The framework is relevant to the International Maritime
Organization's tightening energy-efficiency regulations, where fast, low-cost
exploration of resistance-optimal hulls is a lever for maritime decarbonisation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{S}{hipping} moves roughly \SI{80}{\percent} of global trade by
volume and is responsible for close to \SI{3}{\percent} of anthropogenic
greenhouse-gas emissions~\cite{imo2020,eu2023}. The International Maritime
Organization's Energy Efficiency Design Index (EEDI), the Energy Efficiency
Existing Ship Index (EEXI) and the operational Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII)
have made hydrodynamic efficiency a regulatory obligation~\cite{imo2020}.
Because the resistance of a hull---and hence its installed power, fuel burn and
carbon intensity---is very largely committed during the first weeks of a design
spiral, the early-stage designer's ability to \emph{search} the hull-form space
quickly and cheaply is now a decisive factor in a vessel's lifetime emissions.

Total calm-water resistance is conventionally decomposed into a frictional part,
which is well estimated by standard correlation lines, and a residuary part
(chiefly wave-making) that is far harder to predict and that dominates the
powering penalty at speed. Obtaining residuary resistance traditionally requires
a physical model test in a towing tank or a Reynolds-Averaged Navier--Stokes
(RANS) computational-fluid-dynamics (CFD) simulation. A tank campaign needs a
machined scale model and days of facility time; a single well-resolved RANS
resistance run consumes hours to a day of multi-core wall-clock
time~\cite{molland2011,stern2013}. Neither scales to the $10^{4}$--$10^{5}$
candidate hulls that a global multi-objective optimisation would ideally visit.

Machine learning offers a way out. A \emph{surrogate model} trained once on a
corpus of high-fidelity evaluations returns a prediction in microseconds,
retaining much of the fidelity of its training data~\cite{forrester2008,
queipo2005}. Surrogate-based design is standard in aerospace and automotive
engineering, and a growing body of work applies it to marine
hydrodynamics~\cite{ao2021,cepowski2020,marlantes2023}. Two gaps recur, however:
studies rarely compare modern regressor families \emph{on a common, real
benchmark} under one protocol, and they rarely report \emph{calibrated
uncertainty}---which is exactly what an active-learning or risk-aware design
loop needs.

This paper addresses both gaps using genuinely measured data. We adopt the
Delft Systematic Yacht Hull Series (DSYHS)---\num{308} full-scale towing-tank
experiments~\cite{gerritsma1981,uci}---and make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A real-data benchmark.} We benchmark eight regressors on measured
residuary resistance under one small-data protocol, and against the traditional
polynomial regression historically fitted to this series.
\item \textbf{Calibrated uncertainty.} We show a Gaussian-process surrogate is
both the most accurate model and produces well-calibrated \SI{95}{\percent}
predictive intervals, making it directly usable for active learning.
\item \textbf{Small-data lessons.} We report where models fail (a distance-based
learner collapses) and why, useful guidance for the small datasets typical of
experimental marine data.
\item \textbf{Design-space exploration.} We use the surrogate to screen tens of
thousands of hulls and recover a displacement--resistance Pareto front within
the measured envelope.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys ML across the marine domains.
Section~\ref{sec:problem} formalises the learning problem.
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the data and protocol.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results. Section~\ref{sec:roadmap} sketches a
roadmap, Section~\ref{sec:limits} states limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Machine learning in naval architecture}
Early data-driven resistance work fitted shallow neural networks to the DSYHS
and reported accuracy competitive with regression formulae while extrapolating
more gracefully~\cite{ortigosa2009}. More recent studies use support-vector
regression, ensemble trees and deep networks to predict calm-water resistance,
added resistance in waves and delivered power from hull
descriptors~\cite{cepowski2020,ao2021,kim2020}. A parallel strand couples
surrogates to shape optimisers: Gaussian-process and deep-network surrogates
embedded in genetic and Bayesian optimisation loops have cut the CFD-call count
of hull-form refinement by one to two orders of
magnitude~\cite{marlantes2023,liu2022,serani2016}. We complement this literature
by isolating the surrogate step, comparing regressor families on one real
benchmark, and quantifying predictive-interval calibration.

\subsection{Machine learning in ocean engineering}
Beyond the hull, ML forecasts significant wave height and metocean conditions
from buoy and reanalysis data~\cite{james2018}; recurrent and attention-based
networks predict vessel motions and trajectories from Automatic Identification
System (AIS) streams~\cite{murray2021}; and physics-informed neural networks act
as fast solvers for wave--structure interaction~\cite{raissi2019}. These share
our premise---replacing an expensive evaluation with a learned function---but
operate on time-series and field data rather than static design parameters.

\subsection{Machine learning in shipbuilding and production}
In the shipyard, ML supports welding-defect detection, computer-vision
inspection, predictive maintenance of marine machinery from sensor telemetry,
and assembly scheduling~\cite{lee2021,kong2022}. The digital-twin paradigm
unifies these strands across the vessel lifecycle~\cite{major2021}; a fast
design-stage resistance surrogate is a natural component of such a twin.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Each hull--speed operating point is described by a feature vector
$\mathbf{x}=(\mathrm{LCB},C_p,L/\nabla^{1/3},B/T,L/B,F_n)$: the longitudinal
centre of buoyancy, the prismatic coefficient, the length--displacement ratio,
the beam--draught ratio, the length--beam ratio, and the Froude number. A
physical experiment yields the residuary resistance per unit weight of
displacement, $R_r=f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})$. Because $f^{\star}$ is an expensive
tank test (or RANS run), we seek a surrogate $\hat{f}_{\theta}$ minimising the
expected loss over the design distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star}=\arg\min_{\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p(\mathbf{x})}
\big[\,\ell\big(f^{\star}(\mathbf{x}),\,\hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\big)\,\big].
\label{eq:erm}
\end{equation}
Since $R_r$ is strictly positive and grows almost exponentially with $F_n$, we
train on a logarithmic target $z=\log(1+R_r)$ (multiplicative error structure)
and invert for reporting. Beyond a point estimate we seek a calibrated
predictive distribution $p(R_r\mid\mathbf{x})$: a Gaussian process yields a mean
and variance, from which a $(1-\alpha)$ interval $[\hat{q}_{\alpha/2},
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}]$ is formed and its empirical coverage validated.

For design exploration we use $\hat{f}_{\theta}$ inside a bi-objective search:
at a fixed design Froude number, maximise a displacement-capacity proxy
$c(\mathbf{x})=(L/\nabla^{1/3})^{-3}\propto\nabla/L^{3}$ while minimising
$\hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$. The solution is the Pareto set of non-dominated
hulls,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{x}:\nexists\,\mathbf{x}'\ \text{with}\
c(\mathbf{x}')\ge c(\mathbf{x})\ \wedge\
\hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}')\le \hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\},
\label{eq:pareto}
\end{equation}
with at least one inequality strict.

\section{Data and Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Dataset}
We use the Delft Systematic Yacht Hull Series as published in the UCI Machine
Learning Repository~\cite{uci,gerritsma1981}: \num{308} full-scale towing-tank
experiments performed at the Delft Ship Hydromechanics Laboratory. Each record
gives the six adimensional hull-form parameters of Section~\ref{sec:problem}
together with the measured residuary resistance per unit weight of displacement.
These are \emph{physical measurements}, not synthetic values. The Froude number
spans \numrange{0.125}{0.45} in steps of \num{0.025};
Fig.~\ref{fig:data} shows the characteristic steep growth of $R_r$ with $F_n$
and its modulation by the length--displacement ratio.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{fig1_data.png}
\caption{The DSYHS benchmark: measured residuary resistance $R_r$ against Froude
number for all \num{308} experiments, coloured by length--displacement ratio.
Real towing-tank data.}
\label{fig:data}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Models}
We benchmark eight regressors chosen to span the standard inductive-bias
spectrum and to include the classical baseline for this series:
(i) ordinary least squares; (ii) a degree-3 polynomial ridge regression---the
family of the traditional Delft regression; (iii) $k$-nearest-neighbours
($k=5$); (iv) support-vector regression (RBF kernel); (v) a random forest
(\num{400} trees); (vi) histogram gradient boosting; (vii) a multilayer
perceptron (MLP, $64$--$64$, tanh); and (viii) a Gaussian process (GP) with an
anisotropic radial-basis kernel plus a white-noise term. All models are trained
on the log target through a transformed-target wrapper; scale-sensitive models
are preceded by feature standardisation. The GP additionally returns a
predictive variance used for uncertainty quantification.

\subsection{Protocol and metrics}
At $N=\num{308}$ the evaluation protocol matters more than any single model
choice. We therefore report, as the primary metric, ten-times-repeated ten-fold
cross-validation (\num{100} fits per model), giving the mean and standard
deviation of $R^{2}$ and RMSE in the original $R_r$ units. A single
\SI{80}{}/\SI{20}{} hold-out ($N_{\text{train}}=\num{246}$,
$N_{\text{test}}=\num{62}$) is reserved for the parity plot, permutation
importance and the GP interval-calibration check. We deliberately avoid the mean
absolute percentage error: at low Froude numbers $R_r$ approaches zero, so
percentage errors are dominated by near-zero denominators and become
uninformative; we report $R^{2}$, RMSE and mean absolute error (MAE) instead.
Predictive-interval quality is measured by the empirical coverage of the nominal
\SI{95}{\percent} GP interval on the hold-out set.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Predictive accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the cross-validated metrics and
Fig.~\ref{fig:models} visualises them. Three findings stand out. First, the
\emph{Gaussian process is the most accurate model} by a clear margin, with
$R^{2}=\num{0.999}$ and RMSE \num{0.46}; on the single hold-out it reaches
$R^{2}=\num{0.9996}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:parity}). Second, it \emph{beats the
traditional polynomial regression}---the method historically fitted to this
series---which attains $R^{2}=\num{0.978}$ and RMSE \num{2.04}: the GP reduces
RMSE roughly $4.5\times$. Random forest and support-vector regression are also
strong ($R^{2}=\num{0.995}$ and \num{0.992}), while gradient boosting and the
MLP sit in the middle ($R^{2}\approx\num{0.96}$--\num{0.97}); with only a few
hundred samples the tree- and network-based learners have less room to express
their capacity than the kernel methods.

Third, the \emph{distance-based learner fails}: $k$-NN reaches only
$R^{2}=\num{0.41}$. Because $R_r$ depends so steeply on $F_n$ while the five
geometric features vary independently, Euclidean neighbours in the standardised
six-dimensional space routinely mix very different Froude numbers, so simple
averaging cannot track the response. This is a useful caution for the small,
strongly non-linear datasets typical of experimental marine data: model choice,
not merely model tuning, is decisive.

The tight cross-validation standard deviations (e.g.\ \num{0.001} for the GP)
confirm the ranking is stable rather than an artefact of one split---important
at this sample size.

\subsection{Calibrated uncertainty}
Accuracy alone does not make a surrogate usable inside a risk-aware or
active-learning loop; the model must also \emph{know what it does not know}.
Figure~\ref{fig:calib} plots the GP mean prediction and its nominal
\SI{95}{\percent} predictive interval against the measured values on the
hold-out set. The empirical coverage is \SI{98.4}{\percent}---close to, and
slightly more conservative than, the nominal \SI{95}{\percent}---so the
intervals are trustworthy. This calibrated variance is what enables the
active-learning strategy of Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}, and it is a capability the
tree and network models do not provide out of the box.

\subsection{What the surrogate has learned}
Figure~\ref{fig:importance} shows permutation feature importance for the GP.
The Froude number dominates, consistent with the steep speed dependence of
wave-making resistance; among the geometric parameters the prismatic
coefficient and length--displacement ratio carry the most residual signal, in
line with naval-architecture intuition that fore-and-aft volume distribution
and slenderness govern wave making. Figure~\ref{fig:lc} shows the GP learning
curve: hold-out RMSE falls steeply and then flattens, with most of the
attainable accuracy reached after roughly \num{160} training hulls---directly
relevant to how many physical experiments a new series campaign needs.

\subsection{Surrogate-driven design exploration}
Finally we exercise the surrogate as a design tool. At a representative design
Froude number $F_n=\num{0.35}$, we sample hull-form variants within the measured
parameter envelope and predict $R_r$ for each with the deployed GP. The surrogate
screens \num{60000} candidate hulls at \num{71876}~designs\,s$^{-1}$
(\num{13.9}~\si{\micro\second} per design), and Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the
resulting displacement-capacity--resistance Pareto front. Because the labels are
physical experiments there is no analytic oracle to verify arbitrary new points;
instead the search is confined to the measured envelope (interpolation, not
extrapolation) and its fidelity is bounded by the surrogate's cross-validated
accuracy of Table~\ref{tab:models}. The value is the throughput: each point on
this front would classically demand a separate tank test, whereas the surrogate
renders the entire trade-off in well under a second, turning design exploration
from a campaign into an interactive operation.

\section{Roadmap: Uncertainty-Aware, Multi-Fidelity, Lifecycle}
\label{sec:roadmap}

\paragraph{Active learning}
The GP's calibrated variance (Fig.~\ref{fig:calib}) and the steep early learning
curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:lc}) are the two ingredients of an active-learning loop that
minimises expensive labels: from a small design of experiments, evaluate next the
hull where predictive variance (or expected improvement) is largest, run the tank
test or CFD, and update. This is the established route to one-to-two orders of
magnitude fewer high-fidelity evaluations in hull
optimisation~\cite{serani2016,liu2022}; our calibration result shows the required
uncertainty is trustworthy here.

\paragraph{Multi-fidelity and physics-informed models}
Fusing many cheap empirical or potential-flow labels with a few RANS labels in a
multi-fidelity GP, or embedding known invariants (Froude/Reynolds scaling,
monotonicity in speed) as soft constraints, would extend accuracy beyond the
measured envelope~\cite{raissi2019}.

\paragraph{From design to lifecycle}
A calibrated resistance surrogate is a reusable performance layer for a vessel
digital twin, queried downstream by voyage optimisation, condition monitoring and
EEXI/CII compliance~\cite{major2021}, and the same methodology transfers to the
shipbuilding inspection and maintenance tasks noted in
Section~\ref{sec:related}.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

Four limitations bound the claims. First, the DSYHS comprises sailing-yacht
hulls; although the hull-form parameters and the wave-making physics are general,
transfer to full-form commercial ships must be validated on their own data.
Second, the target is residuary resistance only---frictional resistance is
handled separately by correlation lines and is not modelled here. Third, the
dataset is small ($N=\num{308}$): the high accuracies are credible for this
smooth systematic series and are consistent with the literature, but the
protocol (repeated cross-validation) is what makes them trustworthy, and larger
or noisier data would narrow the model gaps. Fourth, the design exploration
interpolates within the measured envelope at a single Froude number; a
production study would sweep speed, add stability and cost objectives, and verify
selected optima with CFD or a confirmatory tank test. None of these undermines
the core findings---that a calibrated GP surrogate learns measured residuary
resistance to $R^{2}=\num{0.999}$, beats the traditional regression, and enables
fast design exploration---but each marks the boundary of what the present
experiments establish.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Using \num{308} real towing-tank experiments from the Delft Systematic Yacht Hull
Series, we benchmarked eight machine-learning surrogates for residuary-resistance
prediction under a rigorous small-data protocol. A Gaussian process is the most
accurate model ($R^{2}=\num{0.999}$, RMSE \num{0.46}), reduces error roughly
$4.5\times$ relative to the traditional polynomial regression, and---crucially---
produces well-calibrated \SI{95}{\percent} predictive intervals
(\SI{98.4}{\percent} coverage), while a distance-based learner fails, a
cautionary small-data result. The calibrated surrogate screens tens of thousands
of hulls per second to recover a displacement--resistance Pareto front within the
measured envelope. Because its uncertainty is trustworthy, the surrogate is
immediately usable in an active-learning loop that would minimise the number of
costly tank or CFD evaluations---the natural next step, alongside multi-fidelity
fusion and validation on commercial-hull data, toward fast, low-cost exploration
of resistance-optimal, lower-emission hulls.
