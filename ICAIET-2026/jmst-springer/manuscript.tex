\title[ML Surrogates for Ship-Hull Residuary Resistance]{Machine-Learning
Surrogates for Ship-Hull Residuary Resistance: A Benchmark on the Delft
Systematic Series with Calibrated Uncertainty and Design-Space Exploration}

\author*[1]{\fnm{First} \sur{Author}}\email{iauthor@domain}

\author[1]{\fnm{Second} \sur{Author}}\email{iiauthor@domain}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{School of Computer Science and Engineering},
\orgname{XIM University}, \orgaddress{\city{Bhubaneswar}, \postcode{752050},
\state{Odisha}, \country{India}}}

\abstract{Residuary (chiefly wave-making) resistance is the performance quantity
that dominates the powering of a hull at speed, yet obtaining it traditionally
requires a physical towing-tank experiment or a costly Reynolds-Averaged
Navier--Stokes (RANS) computation, so few candidate hulls can be evaluated during
early design. This paper benchmarks machine-learning surrogates that predict
residuary resistance directly from hull-form parameters, trained and evaluated on
real experimental data: the Delft Systematic Yacht Hull Series (DSYHS), 308
full-scale towing-tank measurements. Eight regressors spanning linear,
polynomial, instance-based, kernel, ensemble-tree, neural and Gaussian-process
families are compared under a single small-data protocol (log-target training,
ten-times-repeated ten-fold cross-validation). A Gaussian process is the most
accurate model, reaching a cross-validated coefficient of determination of 0.999
and a root-mean-square error of 0.46 in residuary-resistance units---about a
4.5-fold reduction relative to the traditional cubic polynomial regression
historically fitted to this series (coefficient of determination 0.978)---while
its 95\% predictive intervals are well calibrated (98.4\% empirical coverage). We
also find that a distance-based learner ($k$-nearest-neighbours, coefficient of
determination 0.41) fails on this target, a cautionary small-data result.
Exercised as a design tool, the Gaussian-process surrogate screens tens of
thousands of hull variants per second, recovering a displacement-versus-resistance
Pareto front within the measured design envelope. A multi-fidelity extension,
fusing the cheap traditional regression with scarce high-fidelity points by
input-augmented co-kriging, further reduces error by about a fifth in the
data-poorest regime. The calibrated uncertainty makes the surrogate directly
usable in an active-learning loop that would minimise the number of expensive
tank or CFD evaluations. The framework is relevant to the
International Maritime Organization's tightening energy-efficiency regulations,
where fast, low-cost exploration of resistance-optimal hulls is a lever for
maritime decarbonisation.}

\keywords{Machine learning, Surrogate modelling, Ship resistance, Residuary
resistance, Gaussian process, Uncertainty quantification, Multi-objective design,
Maritime decarbonisation}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Shipping moves roughly 80\% of global trade by volume and is responsible for
close to 3\% of anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions~\cite{imo2020,eu2023}. The
International Maritime Organization's Energy Efficiency Design Index (EEDI), the
Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index (EEXI) and the operational Carbon Intensity
Indicator (CII) have made hydrodynamic efficiency a regulatory
obligation~\cite{imo2020}. Because the resistance of a hull---and hence its
installed power, fuel burn and carbon intensity---is very largely committed
during the first weeks of a design spiral, the early-stage designer's ability to
\emph{search} the hull-form space quickly and cheaply is now a decisive factor in
a vessel's lifetime emissions.

Total calm-water resistance is conventionally decomposed into a frictional part,
well estimated by standard correlation lines, and a residuary part (chiefly
wave-making) that is far harder to predict and that dominates the powering
penalty at speed. Obtaining residuary resistance traditionally requires a
physical model test in a towing tank or a RANS computational-fluid-dynamics (CFD)
simulation. A tank campaign needs a machined scale model and days of facility
time; a single well-resolved RANS resistance run consumes hours to a day of
multi-core wall-clock time~\cite{molland2011,stern2013}. Neither scales to the
$10^{4}$--$10^{5}$ candidate hulls a global multi-objective optimisation would
ideally visit.

Machine learning offers a way out. A \emph{surrogate model} trained once on a
corpus of high-fidelity evaluations returns a prediction in microseconds,
retaining much of the fidelity of its training data~\cite{forrester2008,
queipo2005}. Surrogate-based design is standard in aerospace and automotive
engineering, and a growing body of work applies it to marine
hydrodynamics~\cite{ao2021,cepowski2020,marlantes2023}. Two gaps recur, however:
studies rarely compare modern regressor families \emph{on a common, real
benchmark} under one protocol, and they rarely report \emph{calibrated
uncertainty}---which is exactly what an active-learning or risk-aware design loop
needs.

This paper addresses both gaps using genuinely measured data. We adopt the Delft
Systematic Yacht Hull Series (DSYHS)---308 full-scale towing-tank
experiments~\cite{gerritsma1981,uci}---and make four contributions. First, we
benchmark eight regressors on measured residuary resistance under one small-data
protocol, and against the traditional polynomial regression historically fitted
to this series. Second, we show that a Gaussian-process surrogate is both the
most accurate model and produces well-calibrated 95\% predictive intervals,
making it directly usable for active learning. Third, we report where models fail
(a distance-based learner collapses) and why, useful guidance for the small
datasets typical of experimental marine data. Fourth, we use the surrogate to
screen tens of thousands of hulls and recover a displacement--resistance Pareto
front within the measured envelope.

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Machine learning in naval architecture}\label{subsec:na}
Early data-driven resistance work fitted shallow neural networks to the DSYHS and
reported accuracy competitive with regression formulae while extrapolating more
gracefully~\cite{ortigosa2009}. More recent studies use support-vector
regression, ensemble trees and deep networks to predict calm-water resistance,
added resistance in waves and delivered power from hull
descriptors~\cite{cepowski2020,ao2021,kim2020}. A parallel strand couples
surrogates to shape optimisers: Gaussian-process and deep-network surrogates
embedded in genetic and Bayesian optimisation loops have cut the CFD-call count
of hull-form refinement by one to two orders of
magnitude~\cite{marlantes2023,liu2022,serani2016}. We complement this literature
by isolating the surrogate step, comparing regressor families on one real
benchmark, and quantifying predictive-interval calibration.

\subsection{Machine learning in ocean engineering}\label{subsec:oe}
Beyond the hull, machine learning forecasts significant wave height and metocean
conditions from buoy and reanalysis data~\cite{james2018}; recurrent and
attention-based networks predict vessel motions and trajectories from Automatic
Identification System (AIS) streams~\cite{murray2021}; and physics-informed
neural networks act as fast solvers for wave--structure
interaction~\cite{raissi2019}. These share our premise---replacing an expensive
evaluation with a learned function---but operate on time-series and field data
rather than static design parameters.

\subsection{Machine learning in shipbuilding and production}\label{subsec:sb}
In the shipyard, machine learning supports welding-defect detection,
computer-vision inspection, predictive maintenance of marine machinery from
sensor telemetry, and assembly scheduling~\cite{lee2021,kong2022}. The
digital-twin paradigm unifies these strands across the vessel
lifecycle~\cite{major2021}; a fast design-stage resistance surrogate is a natural
component of such a twin.

\section{Problem formulation}\label{sec:problem}

Each hull--speed operating point is described by a feature vector
$\mathbf{x}=(\mathrm{LCB},C_p,L/\nabla^{1/3},B/T,L/B,F_n)$: the longitudinal
centre of buoyancy, the prismatic coefficient, the length--displacement ratio,
the beam--draught ratio, the length--beam ratio, and the Froude number. A
physical experiment yields the residuary resistance per unit weight of
displacement, $R_r=f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})$. Because $f^{\star}$ is an expensive
tank test (or RANS run), we seek a surrogate $\hat{f}_{\theta}$ minimising the
expected loss over the design distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{\star}=\arg\min_{\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p(\mathbf{x})}
\big[\,\ell\big(f^{\star}(\mathbf{x}),\,\hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\big)\,\big].
\label{eq:erm}
\end{equation}
Since $R_r$ is strictly positive and grows almost exponentially with $F_n$, we
train on a logarithmic target $z=\log(1+R_r)$ (a multiplicative error structure)
and invert for reporting. Beyond a point estimate we seek a calibrated predictive
distribution $p(R_r\mid\mathbf{x})$: a Gaussian process yields a mean and
variance, from which a $(1-\alpha)$ interval is formed and its empirical coverage
validated.

For design exploration we use $\hat{f}_{\theta}$ inside a bi-objective search: at
a fixed design Froude number, maximise a displacement-capacity proxy
$c(\mathbf{x})=(L/\nabla^{1/3})^{-3}\propto\nabla/L^{3}$ while minimising
$\hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})$. The solution is the Pareto set of non-dominated
hulls,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{x}:\nexists\,\mathbf{x}'\ \text{with}\
c(\mathbf{x}')\ge c(\mathbf{x})\ \wedge\
\hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}')\le \hat{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\},
\label{eq:pareto}
\end{equation}
with at least one inequality strict.

\section{Data and methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Dataset}\label{subsec:data}
We use the Delft Systematic Yacht Hull Series as published in the UCI Machine
Learning Repository~\cite{uci,gerritsma1981}: 308 full-scale towing-tank
experiments performed at the Delft Ship Hydromechanics Laboratory. Each record
gives the six adimensional hull-form parameters of Section~\ref{sec:problem}
together with the measured residuary resistance per unit weight of displacement.
These are physical measurements, not synthetic values. The Froude number spans
0.125--0.45 in steps of 0.025; Fig.~\ref{fig:data} shows the characteristic steep
growth of $R_r$ with $F_n$ and its modulation by the length--displacement ratio.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/fig1_data.png}
\caption{The DSYHS benchmark: measured residuary resistance $R_r$ against Froude
number for all 308 experiments, coloured by length--displacement ratio. Real
towing-tank data.}\label{fig:data}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Models}\label{subsec:models}
We benchmark eight regressors chosen to span the standard inductive-bias spectrum
and to include the classical baseline for this series: (i) ordinary least
squares; (ii) a degree-3 polynomial ridge regression---the family of the
traditional Delft regression; (iii) $k$-nearest-neighbours ($k=5$); (iv)
support-vector regression (RBF kernel); (v) a random forest (400 trees); (vi)
histogram gradient boosting; (vii) a multilayer perceptron (MLP, 64--64, tanh);
and (viii) a Gaussian process (GP) with an anisotropic radial-basis kernel plus a
white-noise term. All models are trained on the log target through a
transformed-target wrapper; scale-sensitive models are preceded by feature
standardisation. The GP additionally returns a predictive variance used for
uncertainty quantification.

\subsection{Protocol and metrics}\label{subsec:protocol}
At $N=308$ the evaluation protocol matters more than any single model choice. We
therefore report, as the primary metric, ten-times-repeated ten-fold
cross-validation (100 fits per model), giving the mean and standard deviation of
$R^{2}$ and RMSE in the original $R_r$ units. A single 80/20 hold-out
($N_{\text{train}}=246$, $N_{\text{test}}=62$) is reserved for the parity plot,
permutation importance and the GP interval-calibration check. We deliberately
avoid the mean absolute percentage error: at low Froude numbers $R_r$ approaches
zero, so percentage errors are dominated by near-zero denominators and become
uninformative; we report $R^{2}$, RMSE and mean absolute error (MAE) instead.
Predictive-interval quality is measured by the empirical coverage of the nominal
95\% GP interval on the hold-out set.

\section{Results and discussion}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Predictive accuracy}\label{subsec:acc}
Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the cross-validated metrics and
Fig.~\ref{fig:models} visualises them. Three findings stand out. First, the
Gaussian process is the most accurate model by a clear margin, with $R^{2}=0.999$
and RMSE 0.46; on the single hold-out it reaches $R^{2}=0.9996$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:parity}). Second, it beats the traditional polynomial
regression---the method historically fitted to this series---which attains
$R^{2}=0.978$ and RMSE 2.04: the GP reduces RMSE roughly 4.5-fold. Random forest
and support-vector regression are also strong ($R^{2}=0.995$ and 0.992), while
gradient boosting and the MLP sit in the middle ($R^{2}\approx0.96$--0.97); with
only a few hundred samples the tree- and network-based learners have less room to
express their capacity than the kernel methods.

Third, the distance-based learner fails: $k$-NN reaches only $R^{2}=0.41$. Because
$R_r$ depends so steeply on $F_n$ while the five geometric features vary
independently, Euclidean neighbours in the standardised six-dimensional space
routinely mix very different Froude numbers, so simple averaging cannot track the
response. This is a useful caution for the small, strongly non-linear datasets
typical of experimental marine data: model choice, not merely model tuning, is
decisive. The tight cross-validation standard deviations (0.001 for the GP)
confirm the ranking is stable rather than an artefact of one split---important at
this sample size.

\subsection{Calibrated uncertainty}\label{subsec:uq}
Accuracy alone does not make a surrogate usable inside a risk-aware or
active-learning loop; the model must also know what it does not know.
Figure~\ref{fig:calib} plots the GP mean prediction and its nominal 95\%
predictive interval against the measured values on the hold-out set. The
empirical coverage is 98.4\%---close to, and slightly more conservative than, the
nominal 95\%---so the intervals are trustworthy. This calibrated variance is what
enables the active-learning strategy of Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}, and it is a
capability the tree and network models do not provide out of the box.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering

\caption{Gaussian-process predictive intervals on the hold-out set (sorted by
measured $R_r$). Nominal 95\% intervals achieve 98.4\% empirical coverage---well
calibrated.}\label{fig:calib}
\end{figure}

\subsection{What the surrogate has learned}\label{subsec:interp}
Figure~\ref{fig:importance} shows permutation feature importance for the GP. The
Froude number dominates, consistent with the steep speed dependence of
wave-making resistance; among the geometric parameters the prismatic
coefficient and length--displacement ratio carry the most residual signal, in
line with naval-architecture intuition that fore-and-aft volume distribution
and slenderness govern wave making. Figure~\ref{fig:lc} shows the GP learning curve:
hold-out RMSE falls steeply and then flattens, with most of the attainable
accuracy reached after roughly 160 training hulls---directly relevant to how many
physical experiments a new series campaign needs.

\subsection{Surrogate-driven design exploration}\label{subsec:pareto}
Finally we exercise the surrogate as a design tool. At a representative design
Froude number $F_n=0.35$, we sample hull-form variants within the measured
parameter envelope and predict $R_r$ for each with the deployed GP. The surrogate
screens 60{,}000 candidate hulls at 71{,}876~designs per second
(13.9~microseconds per design), and Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the
resulting displacement-capacity--resistance Pareto front. Because the labels are
physical experiments there is no analytic oracle to verify arbitrary new points;
instead the search is confined to the measured envelope (interpolation, not
extrapolation) and its fidelity is bounded by the surrogate's cross-validated
accuracy of Table~\ref{tab:models}. The value is the throughput: each point on
this front would classically demand a separate tank test, whereas the surrogate
renders the entire trade-off in well under a second, turning design exploration
from a campaign into an interactive operation.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Multi-fidelity fusion with a cheap engineering model}\label{subsec:mf}
Where high-fidelity labels are scarce, a cheap low-fidelity model can be fused
with the few expensive points. We treat the measured residuary resistance as
high fidelity and the traditional Delft polynomial regression---the cheap
engineering formula historically fitted to this series---as low fidelity, fitted
on a disjoint ``historical'' subset so there is no leakage. A multi-fidelity
Gaussian process appends the low-fidelity estimate as an extra input feature, its
automatic relevance determination learning how far to trust it (co-kriging by
input augmentation~\cite{kennedy2000,legratiet2014}). Figure~\ref{fig:mf}
compares single- and multi-fidelity test RMSE as the high-fidelity budget grows.
In the scarce regime---8 tank points---multi-fidelity cuts RMSE by 21\%; the
benefit then narrows and closes once the single-fidelity GP, at roughly 16
points, overtakes the low-fidelity regression itself (dotted line) and no longer
needs it. The lesson is that multi-fidelity pays off precisely where
high-fidelity data is scarce relative to the response complexity---the regime of
expensive commercial-hull CFD rather than this smooth, data-rich yacht series.
This motivates commercial-hull high-fidelity anchors (e.g.\ KCS,
KVLCC2~\cite{tokyo2015}) with coarse-mesh CFD as the low-fidelity source; the
accompanying repository provides the ingestion and feature-space conversion for
such anchors.

\section{Roadmap: uncertainty-aware, multi-fidelity, lifecycle}\label{sec:roadmap}

\paragraph{Active learning}
The GP's calibrated variance (Fig.~\ref{fig:calib}) and the steep early learning
curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:lc}) are the two ingredients of an active-learning loop that
minimises expensive labels: from a small design of experiments, evaluate next the
hull where predictive variance (or expected improvement) is largest, run the tank
test or CFD, and update. This is the established route to one-to-two orders of
magnitude fewer high-fidelity evaluations in hull
optimisation~\cite{serani2016,liu2022}; our calibration result shows the required
uncertainty is trustworthy here.

\paragraph{Multi-fidelity and physics-informed models}
The multi-fidelity result of Section~\ref{subsec:mf} is the first step: extended
to commercial hulls, it would fuse many cheap coarse-mesh CFD labels with a few
fine-mesh RANS or tank labels for anchor hulls such as KCS and
KVLCC2~\cite{tokyo2015,legratiet2014}. Embedding known invariants
(Froude/Reynolds scaling, monotonicity in speed) as soft constraints would
further extend accuracy beyond the measured envelope~\cite{raissi2019}.

\paragraph{From design to lifecycle}
A calibrated resistance surrogate is a reusable performance layer for a vessel
digital twin, queried downstream by voyage optimisation, condition monitoring and
EEXI/CII compliance~\cite{major2021}, and the same methodology transfers to the
shipbuilding inspection and maintenance tasks noted in
Section~\ref{sec:related}.

\section{Limitations and threats to validity}\label{sec:limits}

Four limitations bound the claims. First, the DSYHS comprises sailing-yacht
hulls; although the hull-form parameters and the wave-making physics are general,
transfer to full-form commercial ships must be validated on their own data.
Second, the target is residuary resistance only---frictional resistance is
handled separately by correlation lines and is not modelled here. Third, the
dataset is small ($N=308$): the high accuracies are credible for this smooth
systematic series and are consistent with the literature, but the protocol
(repeated cross-validation) is what makes them trustworthy, and larger or noisier
data would narrow the model gaps. Fourth, the design exploration interpolates
within the measured envelope at a single Froude number; a production study would
sweep speed, add stability and cost objectives, and verify selected optima with
CFD or a confirmatory tank test. The multi-fidelity benefit is itself
regime-specific---largest when high-fidelity data is scarce, and closing on this
data-rich series once the single-fidelity GP surpasses the cheap model. None of
these undermines the core findings---that
a calibrated GP surrogate learns measured residuary resistance to $R^{2}=0.999$,
beats the traditional regression, and enables fast design exploration---but each
marks the boundary of what the present experiments establish.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Using 308 real towing-tank experiments from the Delft Systematic Yacht Hull
Series, we benchmarked eight machine-learning surrogates for residuary-resistance
prediction under a rigorous small-data protocol. A Gaussian process is the most
accurate model ($R^{2}=0.999$, RMSE 0.46), reduces error roughly 4.5-fold
relative to the traditional polynomial regression, and---crucially---produces
well-calibrated 95\% predictive intervals (98.4\% coverage), while a
distance-based learner fails, a cautionary small-data result. The calibrated
surrogate screens tens of thousands of hulls per second to recover a
displacement--resistance Pareto front within the measured envelope. Because its
uncertainty is trustworthy, the surrogate is immediately usable in an
active-learning loop that would minimise the number of costly tank or CFD
evaluations---the natural next step, alongside multi-fidelity fusion and
validation on commercial-hull data, toward fast, low-cost exploration of
resistance-optimal, lower-emission hulls.
